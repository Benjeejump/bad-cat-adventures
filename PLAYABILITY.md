# Playability assessment — v0.12

## Verdict

A strong concept for a personal game: Dylan is the hero, the villains are familiar, the vehicle fantasies matter, and treats buy something he cares about. It works best as a playful adventure with learning encounters, rather than a collection of unrelated activities. It is a playable prototype, with browser verification rather than evidence of a sustained child play session.

## The intended loop

Explore and steer → use a launcher or net → encounter a short challenge → earn treats or words → furnish the puppy home → return to the adventure.

The current update connects encounters to visible causes. Defeating a villain produces a decision game. Bees can be shot or avoided before contact triggers spelling. Bottles require the net and deliver handwriting notes. Timed surprise cutaways and tunnels are removed. The home provides the longer-term goal. The forest adds multiplication practice; breed invitations make the furnished homes feel inhabited.

## Gaps addressed in this release

- **Interruptions:** independent timers previously interrupted ordinary play. Encounters now follow defeats, bee collisions or deliberate pickups. Another activity or menu pauses ambient encounters.
- **Control consistency:** five paw pads are launchers; the HQ house is separate. One D-pad handles car, plane, submarine and walking. Input clears on cancellation, lost focus and menus.
- **Unexpected harmless launcher:** Sonar now chips away at forcefields, so every default launch pad can make battle progress.
- **Pickup ambiguity:** bottles require the explicitly selected net; plain taps do not consume them. They open the note directly instead of another setup step.
- **Store discoverability:** a dedicated Furniture Store entry in HQ opens prices and pictures directly. Purchases place items in the home, affordable items are marked, and the five homes need 10/20/30/40/50 furnishings. Earlier purchased decorations remain saved.
- **Scale:** Pipes uses the workshop floor and larger 24–48-tile grids. The tracing surface fills available space with large formations and a full-note toggle.
- **Reward balance:** defeating a villain earns a treat. Pipes pays five and a completed handwritten note pays eight; longer activities should feel useful. The emergency snack box no longer stacks unlimited free currency.
- **Battle repetition:** defeated enemies get a fresh field for a later appearance instead of repeatedly launching decisions from one already-broken shield.
- **Map complexity:** GPS is retired; the game needs no location permission or mapping connection.

## v0.12 follow-up

- One shared play gate now stops base-world clocks in both the Mission HQ page and modal menus. Bees, projectiles and other world layers are hidden there; home pets and learning activities run on their own clocks.
- Bees have independent starting points, speeds and steering changes. Enemy projectiles are telegraphed and slow enough to dodge or intercept.
- Boosters are visible equipment with activation, recharge, theft and recovery. Missing equipment remains recoverable after reload.
- Furniture and upgrades cost more; the first item is still affordable with the initial balance. Existing purchases remain owned.
- Home occupants walk to furnishings and perform context-specific activities. Breed invitations unlock once and have clear per-home capacity limits.
- The forest offers a small, untimed multiplication loop with concrete grouping hints rather than another speed test.

## Remaining gaps, in priority order

1. **First-session comprehension.** Check whether Dylan discovers the five launcher pads, HQ, net and house without verbal coaching. Prefer one small contextual visual cue over instruction windows. Watch actual behavior before adding more text.
2. **Adaptive learning.** Words have grade settings and supportive hints, but difficulty is not based on a child's recent success. Handwriting checks guide following, not whether an independently written letter is well formed. Do not present it as a handwriting assessment.
3. **Fairness of random decisions.** The requested good/bad choice system deliberately uses chance. A poor result can therefore feel unrelated to effort. Keep setbacks mild and make risky/silly choices legible; learning success should remain dependable.
4. **Long-session variety.** More assets are not the main need. Give each villain a small recognizable pattern, vary routes and puzzles, and introduce new challenge types gradually. Avoid adding more simultaneous controls.
5. **Economy pacing.** Prices and rewards need a real 10–15-minute session. The first affordable furnishing should arrive quickly; later purchases should feel earned without requiring repetitive grinding. The three-treat safety net intentionally still makes low-cost items easy.
6. **Movement purpose.** Dodging bees and collecting treats make steering useful. Car/plane/sub currently share much of the same encounter logic; distinct environments are stronger than distinct missions. Keep mode-specific threats simple and predictable.
7. **Progress and closure.** The house and collected words persist, but there is no clear end to an adventure session. A short optional mission target and a welcoming return-to-HQ moment would help a child stop satisfied.
8. **Tablet comfort and audio.** Browser tests cannot establish physical finger comfort, speech pronunciation or performance on the family tablet. Check landscape and portrait, accidental palm input, low volume and offline reloads.

## Suggested child play session

Play for 10–15 minutes with minimal coaching. Observe whether Dylan can move, hit a villain, avoid or shoot a bee, collect a bottle with the net, finish one puzzle and buy a furnishing. Record where he hesitates, mis-taps or asks what something does. Improve those points before expanding the feature list.
